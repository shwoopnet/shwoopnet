# What a stock strategy would need to reach this bar at our size (Nov 12, 2026). Assumptions from memory and arithmetic, no data used.

The bar: cash PF 1.5 in each of three windows after costs, z 5.46 over the sample. PF 1.5 needs net mean/sd d of about 0.162 per trade. n = (z/d)^2 is about 1,141 independent events for the pooled z, about 255 at z 2.58.

1. EVENTS A YEAR. Over 7.5 years of history, 1,141 events is about 150 independent events a year. A rule that trades every day has 252, so it clears the count; a weekly rule has 52 (390 in the sample, not enough for z 5.46); an earnings rule has about 16 independent earnings weeks a year. So the rule must trade at least about 150 distinct days a year, or the history must be longer than $25 buys.
2. PER-EVENT EDGE. Net mean must be 0.162 x sd. For overnight single-name trades (sd 60 to 100 bp) that is 10 to 16 bp NET; at assumed costs of 3 to 6 bp round trip that is 13 to 22 bp gross, every night. For an index ETF (sd 50 to 70 bp) 8 to 11 bp net, which is more than twice the whole published overnight premium (about 3 to 5 bp). For a 30-minute intraday hold (sd 25 to 35 bp) 4 to 6 bp net, which is more than most published intraday effects. That is why every every-day published anomaly fails.
3. HOLDING PERIOD. The shorter the hold, the smaller the sd and the smaller the edge needed per trade, but the same costs bite harder relative to the edge: cost 3 to 6 bp against a needed gross edge. A hold of minutes needs a gross edge of 5 to 10 bp, which only comes from a forced flow with a predictable direction.
4. WHAT FORCES THE OTHER SIDE. The edge must be paid by someone who must trade regardless of price and cannot trade elsewhere: index funds and ETF baskets at the close, leveraged-ETF rebalances, index additions on the effective date, mandatory sellers after a delisting or merger. Each of those is also known to every proprietary desk, so the question is whether the premium remains after the desks' competition at our fill quality. The edge a small participant can capture is the part the desks leave: names too small or illiquid for them, and then the spread costs (15 bp) eat it.
5. SIZE. At $5,000 a trade, 150 events a year at a net 10 bp is $75 a year. Reaching the bar at our size is possible only in the sense of the statistic; the dollars are trivial unless the account is far larger or the edge is far larger. The same count on a $50,000 position is $750.
6. WHERE TO LOOK, IF ANYWHERE: events that are large (more than 30 bp net each), repeating about 150 times a year, with a named forced seller, in instruments liquid enough to trade at our size without paying more than 3 bp. Candidates that fit part of that: mandatory auction imbalances (S8; frequency and our access are unknown), ETF creation and redemption dislocations in thin ETFs (spread too wide), post-halt or post-delisting forced sales (rare), quarter-end fund window dressing (4 events a year). None has the count and the size together.
Straight answer for the owner: on public liquid US stocks and ETFs the arithmetic leaves essentially one open door, S8 (closing imbalances), and it is gated by access to the offsetting order type and by event counts inside $25. More desk time on stocks is worth it only if S8 shows a LEAD or access is proven; otherwise the stocks search should stop.
